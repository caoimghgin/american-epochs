# The Unravelling Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**The Unravelling (1966–1979)** — Crisis phase of the New Deal Order.

## Epoch identity

The Unravelling is the settlement failing on every front it had claimed to solve.
The coalition that passed the Civil Rights Act split over who it was for. The Cold War
consensus that justified the order's purpose lost a war and then lost the public's
trust in the people who had waged it. The economic machinery that had delivered
rising wages with stable prices stopped working, and the monetary system that
anchored the postwar world was abandoned by the country that had built it. Nothing in
the period was a single collapse. Each failure was a different institution
discovering that the terms of 1933 and 1945 no longer fit, and the fourteen years
read as one long loosening. It ends with a Federal Reserve chairman who has decided
that inflation, not unemployment, is the enemy.

## Historical thesis

The Affluent Society ended at its height and cracked at once, in a riot and a war. The
Unravelling is the crack widening until the order cannot be repaired. Three
fractures run at the same time. **The coalition:** Black Power, the backlash, the
1968 election, and the Southern Strategy break the New Deal majority into
constituencies that no longer share a program, while new claims (women, gays,
Latinos, Native Americans, the environment) arrive faster than the coalition can
absorb them. **The war and the state:** Tet, Kent State, the Pentagon Papers,
Watergate, and the Church Committee teach a generation that the government lies and
that the presidency can be broken. **The economy:** the gold window, wage-price
controls, the OPEC embargo, and stagflation refute the Keynesian claim that
government can manage the cycle, and the order's own central banker ends it in 1979.
The period is also productive: the EPA, Title IX, *Roe*, the Clean Air and Water Acts,
and the Eighteen-Year-Old Vote are real extensions of rights. The unravelling is of
the consensus, not of the country's capacity to legislate. The cycle's crisis is that
the legitimacy that made the order work, the belief that it delivered for the people
who followed its rules, is spent.

## Foundational question

> Can a coalition built on consensus hold the weight of the people it had left out
> and a war it could not win?

The Affluent Society's handoff names both burdens. The Unravelling is the answer.
The excluded ask for what the settlement's terms promised and the order cannot
deliver all of it at once, and the war costs the order its sense of purpose and
its credibility, and the dollar its anchor.

Stress test: the riots, Black Power, and the backlash ask whether the coalition holds the
excluded; Tet, My Lai, Kent State, and the Pentagon Papers ask whether the Cold War
purpose survives the war; the gold window, OPEC, and stagflation ask whether the
economic machinery works without the postwar monetary order; Watergate and the
Church Committee ask whether the state is trusted; Prop 13, the Moral Majority, and
Volcker ask what replaces the settlement. It fits.

## Historical answer

No. The coalition splits in 1968 and does not reassemble. The war ends without victory
in 1973 and 1975. The economy fails the Keynesian test, and the state's credibility
does not recover within the epoch. The settlement's terms survive as institutions
(Social Security, Medicare, the civil rights laws, the regulatory agencies) but its
legitimacy as an order is gone. What the epoch leaves is a vacuum that the next
cycle will fill with a different answer about government.

## Big-picture story

In June 1966 a young SNCC organizer named Stokely Carmichael stood in Greenwood,
Mississippi and called for Black Power, and the civil rights coalition that had
passed two landmark laws in two years started to come apart. The summer of 1967 burned
Newark and Detroit. In January 1968 the Tet Offensive showed that the war's official
optimism was false, and in March Lyndon Johnson told the country he would not run
again. Within ten weeks Martin Luther King and Robert Kennedy were shot. The
Democratic convention in Chicago played out on television as a police riot, and in
November Richard Nixon won with a coalition of the white South, the suburbs, and what
he called the silent majority.

Nixon's first term was ambitious and unstable. Men walked on the Moon in July 1969.
The invasion of Cambodia in the spring of 1970 sent four students to their deaths at Kent
State. The EPA was created and the voting age lowered to eighteen. In 1971 the
Pentagon Papers showed the government had lied about the war for years, and in August
Nixon closed the gold window and froze wages and prices in a single weekend. He went
to China in 1972 and won reelection in a landslide, and the burglary that June at the
Watergate would unmake him. In January 1973 the Paris Accords ended American combat in
Vietnam and the Supreme Court decided *Roe v. Wade*. In October the Saturday Night
Massacre and the OPEC embargo arrived within three days of each other, and
Nixon resigned in August 1974.

Gerald Ford pardoned him, and in April 1975 helicopters lifted the last Americans off
the Saigon embassy roof. The economy had stopped behaving: inflation and unemployment
rose together, which the textbooks said could not happen. New York nearly went
bankrupt. Jimmy Carter, a Georgia governor, won in 1976 by running against Washington
and then spent four years governing in an economy he could not fix. California's voters
capped their property taxes in 1978. In July 1979, after another oil shock and
gasoline lines, Carter gave a speech about a crisis of confidence that the country
heard as a diagnosis and not a plan. The following month he made Paul Volcker
chairman of the Fed. In October Volcker announced that the Fed would stop targeting
interest rates and start targeting the money supply, and the Keynesian era was over.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The coalition breaks, 1966–1968

Black Power, the riots, the Kerner Commission, Tet, Johnson's withdrawal, the
assassinations of King and Kennedy, Chicago, and the 1968 election.

### Act II: The institutions fail, 1969–1974

The Moon landing, Stonewall, Cambodia and Kent State, the EPA, the Pentagon Papers,
the gold window and the Nixon Shock, China, Watergate, Paris, *Roe*, the OPEC
embargo, the Saturday Night Massacre, and the resignation.

### Act III: The economy and the country, 1975–1979

The Fall of Saigon, the Church Committee, New York's near-bankruptcy, Carter's
election, stagflation, Proposition 13, deregulation, Camp David, Three Mile Island,
the Iranian revolution, the hostages, the malaise speech, and Volcker.

## Emotional arc

**Fracture → shock → rupture → loss of trust → exhaustion → reckoning**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Eight point events plus the Watergate and Stagflation spans and the end of the
Vietnam War span:

1. **"Black Power"** (June 1966): the civil rights coalition begins to fracture along the
   line the Affluent Society left unresolved. The live instrument already carries it
   here at Level 1.
2. **Tet Offensive** (January 30, 1968): the war's official optimism fails on
   television, and the consensus that had justified it dissolves.
3. **Nixon elected** (November 1968): the New Deal majority splits, and the Southern
   Strategy and the silent majority begin the realignment that reorders the parties.
4. **Nixon closes the gold window** (August 15, 1971): the dollar leaves the gold standard
   and Bretton Woods ends, the monetary order of the postwar world.
5. **OPEC embargo** (October 1973): the first oil shock reveals that the American
   economy's input costs are no longer American to control.
6. **Nixon resigns** (August 9, 1974): the first presidential resignation, Watergate's
   endpoint, and the order's institutional low.
7. **Fall of Saigon** (April 30, 1975): the war ends in defeat, and the span that began at
   Tonkin closes.
8. **Volcker appointed to the Fed** (August 1979): the regime change that ends full
   employment as the order's priority and opens the next cycle.

Spans: **Watergate** (1972–1974) and **Stagflation** (1973–1979), already in the live
instrument. The **Vietnam War** span ends here (April 30, 1975).

**Spacing check:** the gaps between Level 1 points are about 1.6, 0.8, 2.8, 2.2, 0.8, 0.7,
and 4.3 years. The longest is the four years from Saigon to Volcker. The Stagflation span
runs through it and Level 2 carries Carter's election, Prop 13, Camp David, Three Mile
Island, and the malaise speech. The pair in 1974 and 1975 (resignation and Saigon,
eight months apart) is tight at the top level.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Fall of Saigon** is Level 1. The Vietnam span already ends there, so it could be
  Level 2 with the span carrying the end. I kept it on the crown because the span's end
  is a bar edge and the event is the scene (the embassy roof, the helicopters) the country
  remembers. The cost is two Level 1 points within eight months.
- **King and Kennedy assassinated (April and June 1968)** are Level 2, as in the live
  instrument. Each is a legitimate Level 1 claim and together they are the year's
  shock, but they fall within a year of Tet and the 1968 election, and Level 1 can hold
  only one of 1968's two or three hinges. I picked the war's turn and the realignment.
- **Nixon elected (1968)** is Level 1 on the argument that the realignment is the
  epoch's political spine. The alternative is **Chicago**, which the live instrument has at
  Level 3, or Johnson's withdrawal (March 31, 1968), which belongs with Tet.
- **Roe v. Wade (January 1973)** is Level 2, as live. It reorganizes the parties over the
  following decades, which makes it a candidate for the crown, but within the epoch it is
  one of several new rights claims (Title IX, the ERA, Stonewall) and I kept the crown
  on institutions and the economy.
- **Watergate** is carried by the span and the resignation. The break-in (June 1972) and
  the Saturday Night Massacre (October 1973) are Level 2.
- **Proposition 13 (June 1978)** is Level 2, and the case for promoting it is the
  four-year hole before Volcker. It is the first successful tax revolt and the Reagan
  coalition's rehearsal, but its significance is retrospective. I let the Stagflation
  span carry the gap.
- **The Iranian revolution (January 1979) and the hostage crisis (November 1979).** The
  seizure (November 4, 1979) is Level 2, a point here, and the release (January 20, 1981)
  belongs to Morning in America. The revolution itself is Level 3, which departs from my
  first sketch, because the hostage seizure carries the epoch's last wound and the shah's
  flight is its cause. See the boundary decisions.
- **Carter's "malaise" speech (July 1979)** is Level 2. It is memorable, and it is close
  to Volcker, who ends the epoch.
- **Apollo 11 (July 1969)** is Level 2. It completes the Cold War's great project, the
  Kennedy pledge, at the moment the order's confidence begins to fall away.
- **The environmental statutes (the EPA, the Clean Air Act of 1970, and the Clean Water
  Act of 1972)** are Level 2 as a set. They keep the epoch from being only a story of
  loss: the order's capacity to legislate outlives its legitimacy.
- **Détente (China, SALT I)** is Level 2, and the Cold War span is the live element
  that carries the larger frame.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **Vietnam War (August 7, 1964 to April 30, 1975):** owned by the Affluent Society
  corpus, which begins it. This corpus carries the Paris Accords (January 27, 1973) and
  the Fall of Saigon as end events.
- **Watergate (June 1972 to August 1974):** about two years, already a live span. It
  earns a lane because it is a sustained institutional process, from the break-in
  through the hearings and the impeachment inquiry to the resignation.
- **Stagflation (1973 to 1979):** already a live span at year precision. It is the
  economic condition that defines the period, and it is "necessary but not
  sufficient": the span is a diagnosis, and the events inside it carry the evidence.
- **The Cold War (1947 to 1991):** the live span continues through this epoch.
- **The Iran hostage crisis (November 4, 1979 to January 20, 1981):** 444 days. A
  cross-epoch event of under two years, so a point at the seizure here and a point at
  the release in the next corpus, and not a span.
- **Candidates not taken:** the civil rights movement (a thread, AE-16), the antiwar
  movement (1965 to 1973, overlaps the Vietnam span), the energy crisis (1973 to 1979,
  overlaps Stagflation), and the space race (1957 to 1969, carried by points).

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens January 1, 1966 and follows the Affluent Society's close
  on December 31, 1965. Early 1966 is thin (the Fulbright hearings, the troop buildup),
  and Carmichael's speech in June is the first strong beat.
- **The end.** The corpus closes December 31, 1979. Volcker (August and October) and the
  Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 24) are its last beats. Reagan's election
  (November 1980) opens Morning in America.
- **The Iran hostage crisis** begins here (November 4, 1979) and ends in the next corpus
  (January 20, 1981), as a point at each end, not a span.
- **The Vietnam War span** ends here. The Paris Accords and the Fall of Saigon are carried
  as events.
- **Volcker** is the epoch's closing event and the first of the neoliberal order's
  institutions: the Fed's shift is the order's own central banker acting before the
  electorate does, and the live instrument's note says so. It stays here because the
  decision belongs to the old order's last year and its meaning belongs to the next.
- **Carter's deregulation (1978)** sits here as a Level 3 or 4 event. The airline and
  trucking acts show the next order's logic arriving under a Democratic president, which
  supports the framework's claim that cycles hand off while the old party still governs.

## What the viewer should understand

- The Unravelling is three simultaneous failures (the coalition, the war, the economy)
  that came to look like one.
- The consensus ended before the institutions did. Social Security, Medicare, and the
  civil rights laws all survive, but the order's claim to deliver is gone.
- The period is not only loss: the environmental and rights statutes are among the
  largest of the century.
- The next order's tools (deregulation, tight money, the tax revolt) are visible in the
  last years of this one, and a Democratic administration used the first of them.
- The gold-standard and Keynesian settlements were both abandoned by the people who
  had built them. Nothing external toppled the order.

## Handoff

*By the end of 1979 the settlement still stood as a set of institutions and no longer
as a belief. The coalition had split, the war was lost, the dollar was off gold, prices
and unemployment were rising together, and a president had named a crisis of
confidence and could not cure it. The Federal Reserve was already tightening, and a
tax revolt and a religious movement were assembling a majority. The next question was
whether markets and a smaller government could do what the settlement could no longer
do, and who would be left holding the cost.*
