# The New Freedom Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**The New Freedom (1913–1919)** — first Crisis phase of the Industrial Order, and the first half of its double crisis.

## Epoch identity

The New Freedom was the Progressive Era's institutions arriving all at once and its
confidence dying in the same six years. In eighteen months Washington got a central
bank, an income tax, and a trade commission. Then a war asked more of the country
than any government had before, a peace failed abroad, and the same year brought riots
at home. Americans did not repudiate the institutions. They repudiated what it had
cost to build and use them.

## Historical thesis

Woodrow Wilson's first term completes the institutional program that Roosevelt
improvised. The Federal Reserve, the federal income tax, direct election of senators,
the Federal Trade Commission, and the Clayton Act give the activist state a permanent
legal body. Wilson wins reelection in 1916 on peace, then leads the country into the
European war in 1917 and uses the new apparatus on a scale nobody had imagined: a
draft, controlled prices and railroads, a propaganda office, and prosecutions that
jail dissenters for their speech. The peace he seeks fails in the Senate, the
domestic unity the war demanded collapses into strikes, riots, and a Red Scare, and
Wilson himself is incapacitated by a stroke. The state survives, but the consent it
rested on does not.

## Foundational question

> Having built an activist government, what may it ask of its people, and whose
> freedom does it protect?

The Big Stick left accountability unresolved. The New Freedom puts it to the test twice:
in the question of what a state built to regulate the economy may demand in wartime
(conscription, censorship, nationalization), and in the question of who actually
receives the "new freedom" Wilson promised.

Stress test: the Federal Reserve, FTC, and income tax ask what the state is for; the
draft, the Espionage Act, and the Debs prosecution ask what it may demand; Wilson's
segregation of the federal workforce, *The Birth of a Nation*, the East St. Louis
riot, and the Red Summer ask whose freedom it protects; the suffrage campaign and
the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Amendments ask who shapes the state; Versailles asks
whether it can act abroad on its principles. It fits all of them.

## Historical answer

The institutions endure and the war proves their capacity, but the demands made in
their name (conscription, loyalty prosecutions, suppression of strikes) exhaust
public consent. The country's answer to the question of what government may ask is
"much less." That answer is the next epoch's starting condition.

## Big-picture story

Wilson ran in 1912 on a "New Freedom": break up the trusts' hold on the economy and
open it to small competitors. He took office in March 1913 with a Democratic Congress,
and the first eighteen months were the most productive burst of federal law-making
since Reconstruction. The Sixteenth and Seventeenth Amendments were ratified, a
lower tariff and a federal income tax passed together, the Federal Reserve Act
created a central bank, and the Federal Trade Commission and the Clayton Act gave
government standing tools against monopoly. In the same months, Wilson's
administration began segregating federal workplaces. The "new freedom" had an
unspoken limit.

The European war began in August 1914, and for two and a half years Wilson held the
country out of it. German submarines forced the issue: the *Lusitania* in 1915, the
resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917, and the Zimmermann
Telegram in March. Congress declared war on April 6. What followed was an experiment
in state power with no precedent: a draft, wartime agencies that set prices and ran
the railroads, a Committee on Public Information that shaped what Americans read,
and the Espionage and Sedition Acts, under which people were jailed for speeches and
pamphlets. Eugene Debs got ten years for a speech.

Wilson's January 1918 Fourteen Points were the high-water mark of American
idealism, and the Armistice in November was its triumph. Then everything came apart
within a year. Strikes, riots, and bombs followed the war's end, as the Seattle general
strike, the Boston police strike, a national steel strike, and the Red Summer's race
riots in dozens of cities broke over the country. The Palmer Raids began in November.
The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations, and a
stroke in October left Wilson unable to fight for it. "Safe for democracy" had failed
at home and abroad in the same twelve months.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: Building the New Freedom, 1912–1914

Wilson's election, the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Amendments, the Underwood tariff and
income tax, the Federal Reserve, the Federal Trade Commission, and the Clayton Act.
Federal segregation begins. The Ludlow massacre and the Veracruz landing show the
limits at home and abroad. The Panama Canal opens as the war begins.

### Act II: Neutrality to war, 1914–1917

The *Lusitania*, the Preparedness campaign, and the 1916 progressive legislation
(the Adamson Act, Keating–Owen, the Farm Loan Act) lead to Wilson's reelection on
peace. The Zimmermann Telegram and the resumption of submarine warfare end neutrality.
Congress declares war on April 6, 1917. *The Birth of a Nation*, the Great Migration,
and the suffrage pickets run alongside.

### Act III: The war state and the collapse, 1917–1919

The draft, the Espionage Act, the Committee on Public Information, the Fourteen
Points, and the Sedition Act. The Armistice. Then the failures: the Red Summer, the
1919 strikes, the Palmer Raids, Wilson's stroke, and the Senate's rejection of the
treaty.

## Emotional arc

**Construction → neutrality → mobilization → coercion → idealism → disillusion**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Seven point events:

1. **Federal Reserve Act** (December 1913): the institutional core of the New Freedom,
   the answer to the Panic of 1907 and the Big Stick's unfinished business.
2. **Lusitania sinks** (May 1915): the point at which neutrality stops being a
   choice the United States fully controls.
3. **U.S. enters World War I** (April 1917): the war that makes the activist state a
   war state.
4. **Espionage Act** (June 1917): the domestic cost. The state asks for loyalty and
   prosecutes disloyalty, which is where the epoch's foundational question about what
   government may ask becomes concrete.
5. **Fourteen Points** (January 1918): the high-water mark of the idealism that the
   rest of the epoch undoes.
6. **Red Summer** (1919): the failure at home. White mobs attack Black communities in
   more than two dozen cities, and the "new freedom" is shown to have excluded the
   people who had just fought and worked for the war effort.
7. **Senate rejects the Treaty of Versailles** (November 1919): the failure abroad
   and the epoch's close.

The spine is a rise and a fall: build (1913), drift (1915), commit (1917), coerce
(1917), idealize (1918), fail at home (1919), fail abroad (1919).

**Spacing check** (applied as for the last two epochs): the largest gap between Level
1 points is about two years (December 1913 to May 1915, and May 1915 to April 1917),
and every other gap is under two years. The 1919 pair sits in the epoch's last six
months, so the end of the epoch is crowded and the middle is thin. I think that
mirrors the story, since the collapse was fast, but it is worth checking visually.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **The income tax (Sixteenth Amendment ratified February 1913, Underwood–Simmons
  October 1913)** is Level 2. The Federal Reserve is the better single hinge for
  "built to last," and the income tax's decisive role is as the war's financing, which
  appears at Level 2 in the War Revenue Act. This is the call I am least settled on.
  If you would rather have the income tax in the crown, the strongest swap is the
  Fourteen Points or the Lusitania.
- **Lusitania vs. the Zimmermann Telegram** as the neutrality hinge. I chose the
  Lusitania because it is the moment public opinion turned, and Zimmermann (March 1917)
  is Level 2. Either works.
- **Wilson's reelection (1916)** is Level 2. It is the "He kept us out of war"
  election, but the war declaration five months later is the hinge.
- **Wilson's segregation of the federal workforce (1913)** is Level 2. It is the
  clearest early signal of the question's second clause, "whose freedom," and it
  comes from the administration that coined the "New Freedom." It is a strong
  candidate for promotion if you want the rights thread in the crown. The Red Summer
  carries it in the spine for now.
- **The Armistice (November 1918)** is Level 2. It is the most famous date of the
  epoch, but it is the war's end and not a hinge in this story: the story turns on
  what follows it.
- **Wilson's stroke (October 1919)** is Level 2, folded into the Versailles
  rejection's account. The stroke explains why the treaty failed but is not itself a
  structural turn.
- **Debs's conviction (1918–1919), the Selective Service Act (May 1917), and the
  Clayton Act and FTC (1914)** are Level 2, as the mechanisms of the coercion and
  construction the spine names.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **World War I (1914–1918)** already exists in the live instrument as a span and
  should stay. It is the background condition for most of the epoch.
- **U.S. belligerence (April 1917 to November 1918)** is under two years, so it is
  covered by the war declaration and Armistice as point events.
- **Not spans**: the Great Migration (1916 to about 1970) runs far beyond the epoch,
  and the Progressive reform wave is the epoch itself. The influenza pandemic
  (1918–1919) is about one and a half years and is a point event at its autumn 1918
  peak.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens the day after the 1912 election (Nov 6, 1912), not
  at Wilson's inauguration (Mar 4, 1913). That covers the Sixteenth Amendment's
  ratification (Feb 1913), which the Big Stick outline promised to this epoch, and
  leaves no homeless months between the two corpora.
- **The end.** The corpus closes at the end of 1919. The Palmer Raids began in
  November 1919 and peaked in January 1920. The November raids belong here, and the
  January 1920 raids belong to Return to Normalcy as its opening act.
- **The Nineteenth Amendment** passes Congress here (June 1919) and is ratified in
  August 1920, in the next epoch.
- **Prohibition.** The Eighteenth Amendment is proposed and ratified here (December
  1917, January 1919), but it takes effect in January 1920, in the next epoch.
- **The Federal Reserve** belongs here. The Panic of 1907 is its cause and the Big
  Stick outline already hands it forward.

## What the viewer should understand

- The institutions Wilson built in 1913–14 are the permanent machinery of the modern
  federal government.
- The war used that machinery to ask more of the country than any peacetime
  government had, and the way it asked (conscription, censorship, prosecution)
  damaged the consent it depended on.
- The "new freedom" had an excluded group from the start, and the Red Summer of 1919
  was its bill coming due.
- The retreat that follows is a reaction to this experience, not an accident.

## Handoff

*The institutions Wilson built survived the repudiation of Wilson himself. Having
been asked for everything, in reform and in war, the country wanted to be asked for
nothing. The next question was whether a government designed to govern an industrial
economy could be left alone by the business interests it had been built to restrain,
and what would happen to an economy, and to the people in it, if it were.*
