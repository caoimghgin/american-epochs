# Foundational Editorial Principles

## Purpose

American Epochs is not attempting to catalogue American history. It is attempting
to explain it through progressive disclosure.

The unit of design is not the event. The unit of design is understanding.

## The two jobs of the hierarchy

The highest visible level gives the viewer the **big picture**: the smallest
sequence of events capable of briefly explaining the epoch.

The lower levels do not merely contain less-important facts. They progressively
supply the causes, mechanisms, consequences, counterforces, and human texture
that make the big picture intelligible.

Orientation comes first. Explanation follows.

## Historical thesis and foundational question

Every epoch should have:

1. A **historical thesis**: what changed during this period?
2. A **foundational question**: what underlying constitutional, political, or
   philosophical problem was the nation attempting to answer?

The foundational question is editorial guidance, not a claim that must appear
verbatim in the visualization.

## Event-selection tests

For every candidate event, ask:

- Does removing it make the epoch harder to explain?
- What higher-level event, shift, or condition does it help the viewer understand?
- At what viewing distance does it become necessary?
- Does it clarify the epoch's foundational question?
- Is it an event, or merely a category disguised as one?

Fame alone is insufficient. Category coverage alone is insufficient. Exhaustive
completeness is not the goal.

## Level logic

- **Level 1 — Narrative spine:** the big-picture story visible at the widest view.
- **Level 2 — Major explanation:** indispensable causes, reversals, and consequences.
- **Level 3 — Mechanism:** events showing how the major transformation operated.
- **Level 4 — Context:** political, institutional, social, regional, and personal texture.
- **Level 5 — Fine detail:** incidents that reward the closest inspection.

The useful recursive question is: **Why did this happen?** But the levels are not
a rigid dependency tree. Some events are parallel expressions of the same change;
some are important context without being direct causes.

## The recurring American question

A recurring argument in American history is:

> Who is recognized as a bearer of rights, and who has the authority to protect those rights?

The American proposition treats rights as belonging to humanity rather than as
privileges bestowed by tribal membership. Much of American history can be read
as the repeated conflict between that universal claim and narrower definitions
of who belongs within the protected political community.

## Editorial goal

A viewer leaving an epoch should possess:

1. A concise story of what happened.
2. An understanding of why it happened.
3. A sense of the deeper American argument that gave those events lasting meaning.

Zooming should reward the viewer with better understanding, not merely more facts.
