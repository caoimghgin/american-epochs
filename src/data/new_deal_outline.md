# A New Deal Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**A New Deal (1933–1940)** — Genesis phase of the New Deal Order, the first epoch of the second cycle.

## Epoch identity

The New Deal was an improvised founding. A country answering the collapse of its
market with the most experimental government in its history found out, one decision
at a time, how much of the experiment the Constitution, the courts, and its own
coalition would let survive. By 1940 the answer was "more than anyone had
guaranteed, and less than Roosevelt wanted," and the order that came out of it
carried both its achievements and its limits into the next forty years.

## Historical thesis

Within four years Roosevelt builds the modern American state: bank rescue and deposit
insurance, securities regulation, relief and public jobs, farm supports, the right to
organize, Social Security, rural electrification, and a federal role in housing. The
Supreme Court strikes down the National Recovery Administration and the farm program,
Roosevelt wins a 1936 landslide, and then overreaches with his plan to enlarge the
Court. The plan fails, but the Court yields to the program's constitutional basis
anyway, and the New Deal's legal foundation survives. Then a 1937 recession, a
conservative coalition of Southern Democrats and Republicans, and the war in Europe
end the expansion. The order that survives governs the economy and extends security to
many, but on terms set by the coalition that made it possible, which leaves out
agricultural and domestic workers, and with them a large share of Black and Mexican
American workers.

## Foundational question

> Can a constitutional republic govern its economy at national scale, and who gets
> counted among the people it protects?

The Return to Normalcy handoff asked whether government could be given the power to
govern an economy this large and whether the Constitution, the courts, and the
opposition would allow it. This epoch answers the first half with a qualified yes. Its
second clause is the recurring American question about who is a bearer of rights: the
New Deal's protections were built by a coalition that included Southern Democrats, who
set the price of their votes.

Stress test: the bank holiday and the Hundred Days ask whether government can act at
this scale; the Court fights ask whether the Constitution allows it; the Wagner Act and
Social Security ask what it owes workers; the exclusion of farm and domestic workers
from both, redlining, and the failed anti-lynching bills ask who is inside; the
conservative coalition and the turn to war ask what limits survive. It fits.

## Historical answer

Government can govern the economy, and the Constitution, after a fight, allows it. But
the order that results is bounded by the coalition that built it: expanded security for
those inside its terms and little for those outside, and an expansion that stops in
1937–38 and is replaced by mobilization for war. That is the New Deal Order's
inherited condition.

## Big-picture story

Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, with the banking system closed or restricted
in nearly every state. Within five days Congress passed an Emergency Banking Act in
a few hours, and his first fireside chat persuaded depositors to put their money back.
What followed, the Hundred Days, was the largest burst of lawmaking in the nation's
history: relief, jobs, farm supports, deposit insurance, securities regulation, and an
industrial recovery program that tried to organize business itself.

In 1935 the Supreme Court struck down the industrial program in the *Schechter*
decision and, in 1936, the farm program. The president answered with a second wave
of programs designed to survive judicial review: the Wagner Act, Social Security, and the
Works Progress Administration. In 1936 Roosevelt carried forty-six states. Emboldened,
in February 1937 he proposed adding justices to the Court. His own party revolted, the
plan failed in July, and the Court changed course anyway, upholding the Wagner Act
and Social Security that spring.

That spring was the high point. In the summer the administration cut spending, and a
sharp recession followed. Roosevelt's attempt to purge conservative Democrats from
Congress failed in the 1938 primaries, and Republicans gained eighty-one House seats
in the midterms. A conservative coalition of Southern Democrats and Republicans formed
and blocked new reform for decades. By then Europe was sliding to war. Germany invaded
Poland in September 1939, France fell in June 1940, and by the end of the year the
United States had a peacetime draft, a destroyers-for-bases deal, and a president
elected to an unprecedented third term. The story had stopped being mainly about
America arguing with itself.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The emergency, 1933–1934

The bank holiday, the Hundred Days, the exit from the gold standard, the end of
Prohibition, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the first relief programs. The Dust
Bowl, the Long and Coughlin challenges, and the first labor strikes show the
pressure on the program from both sides.

### Act II: The Second New Deal and the courts, 1935–1937

The Works Progress Administration, *Schechter*, the Wagner Act, Social Security, the
CIO and the Flint sit-down strike, the 1936 landslide, and the court-packing fight and
the Court's change of course.

### Act III: Limits and the turn to war, 1937–1940

The Roosevelt recession, the Fair Labor Standards Act, the 1938 purge and midterms, the
conservative coalition, Kristallnacht and the refugee question, the invasion of Poland,
the fall of France, the destroyers-for-bases deal, the draft, and the third term.

## Emotional arc

**Emergency → experiment → entrenchment → overreach → limits → mobilization**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Seven point events:

1. **Bank holiday and Emergency Banking Act** (March 1933): the rescue. The banking
   system closes and reopens on new terms, which makes the government the lender of
   last resort for the country.
2. **The Hundred Days** (March to June 1933): the founding burst, in which most of the
   agencies and principles of the New Deal appear within a hundred days.
3. **Wagner Act** (July 1935): the right to organize becomes law and leads to the
   CIO and the labor movement that the New Deal Order is built on.
4. **Social Security Act** (August 1935): the permanent welfare state, and the
   program that survives every later attack. It is also where the foundational
   question's second clause is clearest, because agricultural and domestic workers are
   excluded.
5. **Court-packing fails** (July 1937): the contest over survival, in which the
   president tries to override a coequal branch, loses, and wins anyway.
6. **Conservative coalition forms** (November 1938): the midterms hand Congress to a
   bloc that blocks reform and, for decades, civil rights. It is the New Deal Order's
   built-in limit.
7. **Selective Service Act** (September 1940): the first peacetime draft. The story
   turns from domestic argument to mobilization.

The spine is a rise and a boundary: rescue (1933), build (1933), entrench (1935),
entrench (1935), contest (1937), limit (1938), turn (1940).

**Spacing check:** the largest gap between Level 1 points is about two years
(June 1933 to July 1935), and the rest are 1.9, 1.3, and 1.9. The 1933–35 stretch is
filled at Level 2 by the Securities Act, the AAA, TVA, the NRA, and *Schechter*.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Court-packing fails** is Level 1, as in the live instrument. The more consequential
  legal event is probably the Court's "switch in time" (*West Coast Hotel*, March 1937,
  and the Wagner Act decision, April 1937), which is Level 2. I kept court-packing
  because it is the climax of the Genesis question, whether the order survives, and
  because it is the more recognized event. A defensible alternative is to date the
  Level 1 beat to the switch itself.
- **Wagner Act and Social Security as two Level 1 points six weeks apart.** They are
  the order's two pillars, labor and security, but they crowd 1935. I kept both
  because each carries a different part of the foundational question (what government
  owes workers, and who is excluded). The alternative is to fold the Wagner Act into
  Level 2.
- **The conservative coalition** is Level 1. The live instrument has it at Level 3. I
  promoted it because it is the structural hinge between the New Deal's expansion and
  its stall, and because it carries the second clause (the price the Southern wing
  set). The recession (1937–38) is Level 2 as its cause.
- **The Hundred Days** is a range (March 9 to June 16, 1933) of about three months and
  renders as a point at its end, the signing of the National Industrial Recovery Act and
  the Glass–Steagall Act. The bank holiday is its first act and stays separately at
  Level 1.
- **FDR inaugurated (March 4, 1933)** and the **1936 landslide** are Level 2. The
  inauguration's "nothing to fear but fear itself" is the most quoted line of the
  era, but the Emergency Banking Act is the hinge.
- **Schechter (May 1935) and Butler (January 1936)** are Level 2. They are the
  contested-survival thread, and Court-packing's cause.
- **Pearl Harbor**, **Lend-Lease** and **Germany invades Poland**: Poland is Level 2,
  Lend-Lease belongs to the Ascendancy, and Pearl Harbor is far beyond this epoch.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **Dust Bowl (1934–1939)** is the one candidate span: about five years, a
  condition that frames the relief programs, the Okie migration, and *The Grapes of
  Wrath*, and it contrasts with the surrounding political narrative.
- **Not spans:** the Depression's long tail (unemployment stays above 14 percent until
  1941) covers the whole epoch and would relabel it, and the Great Depression span
  already in the live instrument ends in March 1933. The WPA's operating years
  (1935–1943) cross the epoch's end.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens at Roosevelt's inauguration on March 4, 1933. The
  winter's bank panics, the election of 1932, and the Twentieth Amendment are in Return
  to Normalcy, so nothing falls between the two corpora.
- **The end.** The corpus closes on December 31, 1940, which keeps the draft, the
  destroyers deal, the third term, and the "Arsenal of Democracy" speech here. Lend-Lease
  (March 1941) opens the Ascendancy. The live Ascendancy band has a lead-in from 1939,
  which is a drawing convention and does not change which corpus owns these events.
- **Prohibition's repeal** (December 1933) is here, as promised in the previous
  outline.
- **Rights events from 1938–40** (Kristallnacht, the refugee bills, the *St. Louis*) are
  Level 3, tied to the immigration thread, with the 1924 quotas as their cause.
- **The Great Depression span** (live) ends in March 1933 and is not extended.

## What the viewer should understand

- The New Deal was not one program but a series of improvisations, some struck down,
  some replaced, and a few that endured.
- The Constitution bent more than it broke: the Court's retreat in 1937 is what made the
  modern federal government legal.
- The order was built through a coalition that included Southern Democrats, and the
  price they set (exclusions, and no federal action on lynching) shaped who it protected.
- The New Deal did not end the Depression. Unemployment stayed high until the war, which is
  why the stall of 1937–38 matters.
- The story turns from domestic reform to mobilization within months in 1940.

## Handoff

*By 1940 the New Deal had won the argument about what government could do at home and
had run out of room to do more. What carried the order forward was not another reform
but a war, which would ask the new state to do abroad, at far greater scale, what it
had learned to do at home: mobilize an economy, direct its output, and lend its
credit. The question would shift from whether America could govern itself to whether it
would govern the world.*
