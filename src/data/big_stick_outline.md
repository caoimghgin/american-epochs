# The Big Stick Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**The Big Stick (1898–1912)** — Apex phase of the Industrial Order.

## Epoch identity

The Big Stick was the moment America decided that its national government, and
above all its president, should govern: the economy at home and a hemisphere
abroad. Confidence ran ahead of consent. The same energy that regulated meatpackers
and railroads also ruled Filipinos and Puerto Ricans who never voted on it, and the
coalition that built it split over how far it should go.

## Historical thesis

The Gilded Age's unanswered question gets an answer: the national executive and a new
class of federal regulators will govern. Roosevelt breaks up a railroad trust, settles
a coal strike, inspects meat, polices the Caribbean, and builds a canal, and Taft and
the Republican Party follow him to the edge of what that authority can mean. The
same period extends the nation's rule over people outside its electorate (the
Philippines, Puerto Rico, Cuba under the Platt Amendment) and writes segregation
into Southern law and Northern habit. The confidence is real, and so is the
exclusion. It ends in 1912 when the Republican coalition that created the activist
state destroys itself arguing over how activist it should be.

## Foundational question

> Now that the nation has decided its government must govern, who does that
> government answer to, and who is governed without a voice?

The Gilded Age asked who governs. The Big Stick answers (the president, the
regulators, the experts) and inherits the harder question: accountability.

Stress test: trust-busting and the Square Deal ask whom the executive answers to;
the Insular Cases and the Roosevelt Corollary ask who is governed without consent;
the Panic of 1907 shows private finance still answering to no one; Brownsville, the
Atlanta riot, and the founding of the NAACP are the Reconstruction question
continuing under a stronger state; the Progressive Party is a party asking what a
governing president owes his own. It fits all of them without strain, which is what
the test is for.

## Historical answer

The presidency and a federal regulatory apparatus become standing instruments of
national government, at home and abroad. Accountability remains unresolved: the
empire's subjects and Black Americans are governed outside the consent that
justifies the whole project, finance remains largely beyond public control, and the
party that built the system splits over its limits.

## Big-picture story

The war with Spain in 1898 was supposed to free Cuba. Within a year the United
States was fighting a three-year war against Filipino independence fighters, and a
republic that had begun by rejecting colonies governed several million people
overseas who had no vote. In May 1901 the Supreme Court decided the Constitution
did not necessarily follow the flag. That was the Big Stick's foundational
compromise: power would be exercised at a scale the country's own founding
principles did not clearly allow.

In September 1901 an anarchist shot President McKinley, and Theodore Roosevelt
became the youngest president in the nation's history. Roosevelt governed through
the executive: he sued the Northern Securities railroad trust, mediated the 1902
coal strike as a neutral arbiter instead of the strikers' enemy, took Panama, and
declared in 1904 that the United States would police the Western Hemisphere. After
his 1904 landslide he turned to regulation: the Hepburn Act, the Pure Food and Drug
Act, meat inspection. The Gilded Age's weak federal laws got teeth.

It did not last. The Panic of 1907 showed that the regulation had not touched
finance: J.P. Morgan again rescued the banking system, as in 1895. Roosevelt's chosen
successor, William Howard Taft, kept the program but not the temperament, and
Roosevelt came back in 1910 with a "New Nationalism" more radical than anything he
had done as president. In 1912 he bolted the Republican convention and ran against
Taft. The vote split three ways, and Woodrow Wilson won with a plurality of 42 percent.
The coalition that built the Big Stick had destroyed itself from pride, not opposition.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: Empire, 1898–1901

The Spanish–American War, the annexation of Hawaii, the Treaty of Paris, and the
Philippine War extend American rule overseas. The Open Door notes claim a stake in
China. The Insular Cases settle the constitutional status of the new possessions.
McKinley's assassination ends the act.

### Act II: Roosevelt and the executive state, 1901–1908

Roosevelt turns the presidency into the engine of national government. Northern
Securities, the anthracite strike, the Department of Commerce and Labor, Panama,
the Roosevelt Corollary, the Hepburn Act, and the Pure Food and Drug and Meat
Inspection Acts all come from the executive. Alongside them: Jim Crow hardens,
the Brownsville affair and the Atlanta riot mark the limits of Roosevelt's
racial politics, and the Niagara Movement forms in response. The Panic of 1907
shows the limit of the program's reach.

### Act III: The coalition splits, 1909–1912

Taft inherits the program and the party's factions. The Payne–Aldrich tariff and the
Ballinger–Pinchot affair open the fight between Republican Progressives and
Old Guard. Roosevelt's "New Nationalism" speech in 1910 breaks with Taft's version of
the party. In 1912 the Republican convention renominates Taft, Roosevelt founds the
Progressive Party, and Wilson wins.

## Emotional arc

**Confidence → expansion → regulation → overreach → fracture**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Eight point events plus one span:

1. **Spanish–American War** (1898): the country becomes an overseas power and
   acquires subjects who have no vote.
2. **McKinley assassinated** (September 1901): Roosevelt, and with him the activist
   presidency, arrives.
3. **Northern Securities suit** (February 1902): the executive, not Congress or the
   courts alone, starts breaking up concentrated capital. It answers the Gilded Age's
   unanswered question directly.
4. **Roosevelt Corollary** (December 1904): the president claims a police power over
   the hemisphere, the foreign-policy side of the same governing impulse.
5. **Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act** (June 1906): regulation reaches
   what Americans eat. The high-water mark of the Square Deal.
6. **Panic of 1907** (October 1907): the limit. A private banker rescues the system
   again, and the lesson leads straight to the Federal Reserve in the next epoch.
7. **"New Nationalism" speech** (August 1910): Roosevelt's break with Taft's party,
   the start of the fracture.
8. **Election of 1912** (November 1912): the coalition destroys itself, and Wilson
   wins with a plurality. It closes the epoch.

Span: **Philippine–American War** (1899–1902), the scene-setting background of
empire.

**Spacing check** (applied after the Gilded Age's ten-year hole in Level 1): the
largest gap between Level 1 points is 3.5 years (1906 to 1910), and Panic of 1907
sits inside it. No stretch is more than about three years from a hinge.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **The Insular Cases** (1901) are Level 2, not Level 1. They are the constitutional
  statement of the empire, which makes them the strongest candidate for promotion
  if you want the epoch's foundational question ("who is governed without a voice")
  visible in the crown. The war already marks the empire, so I left them out of the
  spine. This is the call I'm least settled on.
- **Panic of 1907** is Level 1 partly for spacing and partly because it shows the
  limit of Progressive regulation and sets up the Fed. Without it, the spine would
  leave 1906 to 1910 empty and the story would read as unbroken triumph until
  Taft. A defensible alternative is the Hepburn Act or the Great White Fleet.
- **Roosevelt Corollary** is Level 1 because it is the foreign-policy half of the
  executive-state argument and is the best-known statement of "big stick"
  diplomacy. The Panama Canal is Level 2: it is the Corollary's most visible result.
- **The NAACP's founding (1909) and Brownsville (1906)** are Level 2. They are the
  rights thread, and the same reasoning that kept Plessy at Level 2 in the Gilded Age
  applies: it answers the Reconstruction question, not this epoch's. Their weight
  shows in the foundational question's second clause.
- **Hepburn Act** is Level 2. It finally gave the Interstate Commerce Commission rate-setting
  authority and is the clearest callback to the Gilded Age's Interstate Commerce Act.
- **Taft's election (1908), Payne–Aldrich (1909), and Ballinger–Pinchot (1909–10)** are
  Level 2–3: the causes of the split, not hinges of it.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **Philippine–American War (1899–1902)**: yes. About three years, and it
  contrasts with the confident narrative around it.
- **Not spans**: the Great Merger Movement (1895–1904) is about half the epoch and
  predates it, the peak immigration years run past the epoch's end, and "Progressive
  Era" as a label covers all of it. Each appears as point events instead (U.S. Steel,
  the Dillingham Commission, the Triangle fire).

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens with the sinking of the USS *Maine* (Feb 15,
  1898), the trigger, with the declaration of war (Apr 25, 1898) as the first
  Level 2 beat. The live band starts in 1898, so this fits. This leaves about eleven
  months of 1897 (after McKinley's inauguration on March 4) belonging to neither
  epoch in the corpus files. The live bands are whole-year and don't show a gap, and
  nothing structural happens in that window (the Dingley Tariff and prosperity's
  return are the main items). Open question for you: leave it, or extend the Gilded
  Age's end to December 31, 1897?
- **The end.** The corpus closes at Election Day, Nov 5, 1912. Wilson's inauguration
  (Mar 4, 1913) belongs to the New Freedom.
- **Hawaii's overthrow (1893)** is already in the Gilded Age at Level 4 and the
  annexation (July 1898) belongs here.
- **The Sixteenth Amendment** is proposed here (July 1909) but ratified in the New
  Freedom (Feb 1913). The proposal is Level 3 in this corpus; the ratification belongs
  to the next.
- **The Federal Reserve** is not in this epoch. The Panic of 1907 and the Aldrich plan
  are its causes, and the Act itself (Dec 1913) opens the New Freedom.

## What the viewer should understand

- The Gilded Age's question got an answer: the national government, led by the
  presidency, would govern the industrial economy and the hemisphere.
- That answer was built on personal authority and was far from universal. The empire's
  subjects and Black Americans were governed outside the consent that justified the
  project.
- Regulation reached products and railroads but not finance, and the Panic of 1907 showed it.
- The collapse in 1912 came from the Republican coalition's own divisions, not from an
  outside defeat, which is why it reads as a peak and a crack in one.

## Handoff

*Theodore Roosevelt had proved that a president could govern an industrial nation
and police a hemisphere, but he had done it on personal authority, through a party
that could not hold together once he left it. The country's next great question would
be whether what he had improvised could be built to last, in law, in the Treasury, and
in a Congress willing to share the work, before a war arrived to test what the new
government was willing to ask of its people.*
