# Return to Normalcy Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**Return to Normalcy (1920–1932)** — second Crisis phase of the Industrial Order, and the second half of its double crisis.

## Epoch identity

Return to Normalcy was the decade America tried to be left alone: by its government,
by Europe, by reform. It discovered that being left alone meant the same ideology that
built the boom had no answer when the boom broke. It is one story, not two. The
prosperity and the Depression were produced by the same refusal to govern the economy,
and the people left outside the prosperity (farmers, Black Americans, immigrants)
were the first to show it.

## Historical thesis

After the New Freedom's war and its repudiation, the national government steps back.
Harding, Coolidge, and Hoover cut taxes on the wealthy, raise tariffs, staff the
regulatory agencies with friends of the industries they oversee, close the door on
immigration, and decline to help farmers. The economy booms on automobiles, radio,
electric appliances, installment credit, and a stock market bought on margin, while
wealth concentrates and agriculture is in depression from 1920 onward. When the boom
breaks in 1929 the same ideology refuses a counterweight: Hoover relies on voluntary
cooperation, signs the Smoot–Hawley tariff, and acts too late with too little. By
1932 a quarter of the workforce is unemployed, the banks are failing, and the
Army has driven its own veterans out of Washington.

## Foundational question

> When government steps back from governing the economy, what does it still insist
> on governing, and who is left outside?

The New Freedom asked what government may demand of its people. Normalcy asks the
reverse, and finds an irony. The decade's government retreats from the economy,
leaving it to business as if the market were natural, and advances on membership:
quotas, consular visas, a Border Patrol, a Bureau of Investigation. It treats the
nation as natural too, an inherited people, and both are built and enforced by the
state. The second clause carries the recurring American question about who is
protected, since the same decade closes the immigration door, revives the Klan,
and leaves farmers and Black Southerners to the market.

Stress test: Teapot Dome and the tax and tariff policy ask whom government serves;
the boom and the Crash ask who answers for the economy; the 1924 immigration law,
the Klan's peak, Tulsa, Scopes, and Sacco and Vanzetti ask who is left outside;
McNary–Haugen asks the same of farmers; Hoover's response, the Bonus Army, and
the 1932 election ask what the state owes people when the market fails. It fits.

## Historical answer

Business governance fails. The ideology that inflated the boom produces no counterweight
when it turns, and by 1932 the country repudiates it at the ballot box. What
replaces it is not yet settled, which is the definition of the second crisis of a
double crisis: the first (the New Freedom) exposed the old order's failure, and the
second is what happens while nothing yet replaces it.

## Big-picture story

Warren Harding ran in 1920 on "a return to normalcy," and won by the largest
margin in a century. What the country wanted back was a government that asked little
of it. Harding and then Coolidge delivered: the budget was cut, Andrew Mellon's
Treasury cut taxes on the top brackets, the tariff went up, and the regulators were
friendly. The Teapot Dome scandal showed where that could lead, with oil leases
traded for cash, but the scandals did not dent the voters' approval.

The decade's prosperity was real and uneven. The automobile, radio, and the
appliance transformed daily life, paid for by installment credit. A stock market
bubble began around 1924 and swelled on borrowed money. Farm prices collapsed after
the war and never recovered, and Congress passed the McNary–Haugen farm relief bill
twice, only for Coolidge to veto it. Black Americans moved north by the hundreds of
thousands and met new forms of exclusion. In 1924 Congress wrote national-origin
quotas into law. The Klan peaked in the middle of the decade. The Scopes trial
staged the fight between old and new America in a Tennessee courtroom.

Coolidge told editors in 1925 that "the chief business of the American people is
business," and in 1928 Herbert Hoover, the engineer who had become a national hero
for relieving famine and flood, won the presidency by a landslide, promising that
poverty would soon be abolished. The Crash came eleven months later. Hoover's
response was to rely on voluntary cooperation, to sign the highest tariff in
history, and to create the Reconstruction Finance Corporation only in 1932, after
three years of falling output, bank failures, and unemployment. In July 1932 the Army
cleared the Bonus Army of veterans from Washington with tear gas and bayonets, and
the decade's idea of normalcy ended with it. In November the country elected Franklin
Roosevelt.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The retreat from Wilson, 1920–1923

Harding's election, the end of the wartime state, tax cuts, the tariff, and the
Budget Act. The postwar recession of 1920–21. The Tulsa massacre, Sacco and
Vanzetti's convictions, and the immigration quotas show who was being left out.
Teapot Dome and Harding's sudden death in 1923 close the act.

### Act II: The boom and its shadows, 1923–1928

Coolidge's presidency, the 1924 election, the Immigration Act, the Indian
Citizenship Act, the Klan's peak, Scopes, the Harlem Renaissance, radio and the
automobile, Lindbergh, the farm crisis and the McNary–Haugen vetoes, and the
speculative Florida land boom and bust. Hoover wins in 1928.

### Act III: The bust and the denial, 1929–1932

The Crash, Smoot–Hawley, the bank failures, Hoover's voluntarism and the moratorium,
the RFC, Hoovervilles and hunger marches, the Bonus Army, and the election of 1932.

## Emotional arc

**Retreat → exclusion → boom → denial → collapse → repudiation**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Eight point events plus the existing Great Depression span:

1. **Harding elected** (November 1920): the country chooses "normalcy," and the
   epoch gets its name and its first plank, a government that asks little.
2. **Immigration Act of 1924** (May 1924): the door closes. National-origin quotas are
   the epoch's clearest answer to who is left outside, and the first law to set
   national-origin quotas on European immigration. It changes the individual's
   relationship to the state: before 1921 admission turned on individual
   characteristics (health, means, politics), and after it a person's fate was
   set by ancestry and the quota's remaining slots, with no category for flight
   from persecution. It sets the base census at 1890, before the Southern and Eastern
   European wave, so the law works as nostalgia for an earlier America. Because the
   Western Hemisphere is exempt and European labor is cut off, employers turn
   to Black Southerners and Mexican workers, which sustains the Great Migration.
3. **"The business of America is business"** (January 1925): the ideology stated by
   the president who embodies it.
4. **Coolidge vetoes McNary–Haugen** (February 1927): the government declines to act
   for the sector already in depression, which is the first sign that the boom has
   losers and that the state will not intervene.
5. **Hoover elected** (November 1928): normalcy at its peak, a landslide on promised
   abolition of poverty. It is the epoch's apex and its irony.
6. **Stock market crash** (October 1929): the boom breaks.
7. **Smoot–Hawley Tariff** (June 1930): the ideology's response to the crisis, which
   deepens it. It is the clearest case of the absence of a counterweight.
8. **Bonus Army** (July 1932): the Army drives its own veterans from Washington. The
   decade's idea of normalcy ends with its force turned on its own.

Span: **Great Depression** (August 1929 to March 1933), already in the live instrument.

**Spacing check:** the gaps between Level 1 points are about 3.5 years (1920 to 1924),
about 0.7 years, about 2.1 years (1925 to 1927), under 2 years each from there to
1929, and 2.1 years (1930 to 1932). The longest gap is the opening one, 1920 to 1924,
which Teapot Dome, the 1921 tax cut, and Tulsa fill at Level 2. The boom years
are the quiet center of the decade, and the spine reflects that honestly: the
boom is a condition, not an event.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Immigration Act of 1924** is Level 1. The live instrument has it at Level 3. I
  promoted it because it is the epoch's clearest hinge for the foundational
  question's second clause, and because it is the single most consequential law of
  the period. The case against is that it belongs to the rights thread, which I kept
  at Level 2 in earlier epochs on the grounds that it answers a different epoch's
  question. Here it does not: the question's second clause is about who is left outside.
- **McNary–Haugen veto** is Level 1 partly for spacing and partly because the farm
  crisis is the earliest and most visible sign that "normalcy" had losers. It is an
  unusual choice for a crown, since a veto is not a famous event. Alternatives are the
  Scopes trial (famous but off-argument) and Lindbergh (famous and irrelevant).
- **Smoot–Hawley** vs. the **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** (January 1932). I kept
  Smoot–Hawley because it is the ideology's response to the crisis; the RFC is the
  first sign of its retreat. A defensible alternative reading puts the RFC in the
  crown instead, as the hinge to the New Deal. It is Level 2.
- **Teapot Dome** is Level 2. It was the era's emblem of government for sale, but
  the voters did not punish it, so it does not carry the causal chain.
- **Election of 1932** is Level 2. The Bonus Army is the epoch's closer, as in the
  live instrument, and the election is its consequence. It is also the first beat of
  the next epoch's story.
- **Prohibition** (January 1920) is Level 2. It is the decade's most famous policy,
  but it is a failed attempt at the state governing private life, and it sits
  slightly beside the epoch's argument about the economy.
- **Scopes trial, Tulsa, Sacco and Vanzetti** are Level 2 or 3 as the exclusion thread.
- **The Great Migration's second wave** (about 800,000 people in the 1920s) is Level 2
  in this corpus, with the 1916 start already in the New Freedom. It began with
  the war's cutoff of European labor and was locked in by the quotas. The Harlem
  Renaissance (Level 3) is its cultural result.
- **Cross-epoch thread.** The immigration thread runs through the Chinese Exclusion
  Act (1882, Gilded Age), the Gentlemen's Agreement (1907, Big Stick), the 1921 and
  1924 acts, and the 1965 abolition of national origins (a later epoch). It is a
  candidate for a future cross-epoch "threads" feature, and the YAML tags already
  make it queryable.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **Great Depression (August 1929 to March 1933):** already in the live instrument
  and kept. It runs past the epoch's last event and into the Hundred Days, which
  makes it a deliberate boundary exception: it is the shared background of this
  epoch's last three years and the New Deal's opening weeks. The Gilded Age's
  depression span was clipped at its epoch's edge, but there the epoch boundary came
  at an arbitrary point in a long recovery, and here the NBER dates the trough to the
  month the New Deal begins.
- **Not spans:** Prohibition (1920–1933) and the farm depression (1920–1933) each
  cover the whole epoch and would read as relabeling it. The 1920s stock market boom
  (about 1924–1929) has no crisp start and is carried by point events (the
  1927 rate cut, the 1929 crash). The Klan's mid-decade peak is a point-event
  cluster.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens on January 1, 1920, which puts the January 1920
  Palmer Raids (the wave the New Freedom outline handed forward) and Prohibition's start
  here. The Nineteenth Amendment's ratification (August 1920) is here, as promised.
- **The end.** The corpus closes at the end of Hoover's term, March 3, 1933, so the
  election of 1932, the winter's bank panics, and the Twentieth Amendment's ratification
  (January 1933) belong here as the epilogue. A New Deal opens with the bank holiday.
  The live bands are whole-year, so this does not change what the chart shows.
- **Bonus Army** is the closing Level 1 beat, as in the live instrument. The
  election and the banking collapse that follow are Level 2 and below.
- **Prohibition** begins here and is repealed in December 1933 in the New Deal's
  first year.
- **The Great Depression span** crosses the boundary on purpose (see above).

## What the viewer should understand

- "Normalcy" was a policy of governmental retreat, not an absence of policy.
- The boom and the bust are one story. The refusal to govern the economy made both.
- The prosperity was real and narrow: farmers, Black Americans, and immigrants were
  left outside it from the start.
- Business governance did not fail because of a single mistake in 1929. It failed
  because it had no mechanism for responding when the market broke.
- The 1932 election was a repudiation of an ideology, which leaves the question of
  what replaces it open.

## Handoff

*The country had tried leaving the economy to those who owned it, and in four years
that experiment ended in closed banks, breadlines, and soldiers clearing their own
veterans from the capital. Normalcy had been repudiated, but nothing had yet
replaced it. The next question was no longer whether government should govern an
economy this large, but whether it could be given the power to, and whether the
Constitution, the courts, and the opposition would let it.*
