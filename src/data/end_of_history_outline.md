# The End of History Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**The End of History (1992–2007)** — Apex phase of the Neoliberal Order.

## Epoch identity

The End of History is the epoch in which the order had no rival and believed it. The
Soviet Union was gone, the opposition party had accepted the order's terms, the economy
boomed, the budget briefly balanced, and a generation of serious people concluded that
liberal democracy and open markets were the endpoint of ideological argument. It was a
sincere belief, held for fifteen years. The epoch tests it against four events: a
disputed election decided by the Supreme Court, the largest attack on American soil, a
war begun on a premise that proved false, and a drowned city that the state could not
rescue. The confidence died before the crisis. By the summer of 2007, with Iraq soured,
New Orleans a ruin, and housing prices falling, the belief was already spent, and the
financial collapse that followed named it.

## Historical thesis

Morning in America built the order and the Cold War's end proved it. The End of History is
the order at its apex. **The ratification** comes first: Clinton wins in 1992 on the
economy, then governs inside Reagan's terms, with "the era of big government is over,"
NAFTA, the 1996 welfare law, a balanced budget, and the repeal of Glass–Steagall. The
opposition party has accepted the settlement, as Eisenhower accepted the New Deal. **The
boom** is real: the longest expansion in American history to that point, unemployment
near 4 percent, a budget surplus, the internet, and gains that reached the bottom of
the wage scale for a few years. **The gains do not spread evenly.** China's entry into
the trade system, deindustrialization, financialization, and rising household debt move
the benefits toward capital and the educated, and the costs toward particular regions.
**The state's confidence breaks in public.** *Bush v. Gore* shows the Court deciding an
election, the Patriot Act, Guantánamo, and the torture memos extend executive power
around the law, the Iraq War's premises fail, and Katrina exposes the state's incapacity
at home. The epoch ends with the housing peak and the first credit failures, the beginning
of the order's collapse rather than its cause.

## Foundational question

> Can an order with no rival left share its gains, and which party will claim to be its
> steward?

The Morning in America handoff names both. The first is whether the winners' order
distributes what it produces. The second is the political question of who governs
inside it, and the answer is that both parties do.

Stress test: Clinton's election, NAFTA, welfare reform, and the 1998 budget surplus ask
who stewards the order and whether it is shared; Glass–Steagall's repeal, the China trade
agreement, and the dot-com boom ask where the gains go; *Bush v. Gore*, the Patriot Act, and
the torture memos ask what the order does with executive power; 9/11 and Iraq ask whether
a power with no rival can use force without cost; Katrina and the housing bubble ask
whether the order's institutions work. It fits.

## Historical answer

Partly, and for a while. Both parties came to steward the order, and its gains were shared
more widely in the late 1990s than at any time since the 1960s. After 2000 the gains
narrowed, the costs of the war and the trade shock were borne by particular people, and the
state's competence failed at the moments it was tested. The order's creed (open markets,
American power, expert management) held longer than its results.

## Big-picture story

George H. W. Bush won the Gulf War and lost the 1992 election to a governor from Arkansas
who ran on the economy and a ballot on which Ross Perot took 19 percent. Bill Clinton
came into office as a New Democrat. He raised taxes on the wealthy in 1993, signed NAFTA
that year over the objections of his own party's labor wing, and watched the Republicans
take Congress in 1994 for the first time in forty years. In 1996 he told the nation that
"the era of big government is over" and signed a law ending the federal guarantee of
welfare. The economy boomed on the internet, a rising stock market, and falling
unemployment, and by 1998 the budget was in surplus. Clinton was impeached for lying about
an affair and acquitted. In 1999 he signed the repeal of Glass–Steagall, and in 2000 he
signed permanent normal trade relations with China.

The 2000 election was decided by thirty-six days of recounts and a 5-to-4 Supreme Court
ruling that stopped them, and George W. Bush took office having lost the popular vote.
Eight months later, on September 11, 2001, nineteen hijackers killed nearly three
thousand people. The country invaded Afghanistan within a month, passed the Patriot Act, and
built a legal structure of detention and surveillance around the executive. In March 2003
it invaded Iraq on the claim that Saddam Hussein held weapons of mass destruction. They
were not found, and the occupation, the insurgency, and the photographs from Abu Ghraib
followed. In 2005 Hurricane Katrina drowned New Orleans, and a federal response that took
days became the epoch's other lasting image. In 2006 housing prices peaked. In the summer
of 2007 two Bear Stearns funds collapsed and a French bank froze redemptions in its own, and
a year later the order that Fukuyama had named came apart.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The ratification, 1992–1996

Fukuyama's book, the Los Angeles riots, Clinton elected, NAFTA, the Gingrich revolution,
the shutdown, Oklahoma City, welfare reform, and the Telecommunications Act.

### Act II: The boom, 1997–2000

The Asian crisis, LTCM, the surplus, impeachment, Kosovo, the dot-com peak, Glass–Steagall's
repeal, China's trade status, and *Bush v. Gore*.

### Act III: The shock and the overreach, 2001–2007

September 11, Afghanistan, the Patriot Act, Enron, the Iraq War, Abu Ghraib, Katrina,
the housing peak, the surge, and the first credit failures.

## Emotional arc

**Ratification → boom → dispute → shock → overreach → doubt**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Nine point events plus two spans (the editor's call: Gramm–Leach–Bliley and China PNTR both go to Level 1, and Afghanistan becomes a span beside Iraq):

1. **Clinton elected** (November 3, 1992): the opposition accepts the order, the
   framework's ratification signal. Clinton wins on the economy, and Perot takes 19
   percent.
2. **NAFTA** (signed December 8, 1993): free trade becomes a Democratic project, passed
   over the objections of the party's labor wing.
3. **Welfare reform** (August 22, 1996): a Democratic president ends the federal guarantee
   of cash assistance, and the order's terms are written into the safety net.
4. **Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act** (November 12, 1999): the repeal of Glass–Steagall's wall
   between commercial and investment banking, signed by Clinton, with the Commodity
   Futures Modernization Act following in 2000. The financial system's rules are rewritten
   for the winners.
5. **China PNTR** (October 10, 2000): the trade opening that pairs with the financial one. Added on the editor's call; it takes effect with WTO entry on December 11, 2001.
6. **Bush v. Gore** (December 12, 2000): the Supreme Court halts the Florida recount and
   decides a presidential election, and the order's legitimacy rests on the Court's.
7. **September 11** (2001): the largest attack on American soil begins the war on
   terror and a new era of executive power.
8. **Invasion of Iraq** (March 20, 2003): the order's confidence in force and its premise
   fails together, and the span that runs into the next epoch begins.
9. **Hurricane Katrina** (August 29, 2005): a major American city drowns, and the state
   is shown unable to protect its own.

Spans: the **Afghanistan War** (October 7, 2001 to August 30, 2021) ends in the Reckoning, and the **Iraq War** (March 20, 2003 to December 18, 2011) begins here. It is owned by
this corpus and runs about four years into the Interregnum, which will carry its end
events. The live placeholder is year-level and will be made precise.

**Spacing check:** the gaps between Level 1 points are about 1.1, 2.7, 3.2, 1.1, 0.8, 1.5,
2.4, and a close of 2.3 years to the end of the epoch. The longest is the three years from
welfare reform to Glass–Steagall, filled at Level 2 by the Gingrich revolution's aftermath
(the shutdown), Oklahoma City, the Asian crisis, impeachment, and Kosovo. The back half is
dense, which reflects the epoch: the first half is a settlement and the second is a series of
tests.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Glass–Steagall's repeal vs China's trade status.** I put the financial law on the crown
  because it is the order's own deregulation under a Democratic president and the seed
  of the crash. China's permanent normal trade relations (October 2000) and its entry to
  the WTO (December 2001) are the strongest alternative: they reordered American
  manufacturing and may be the most consequential economic act of the epoch. The Level 2
  note can carry them. I would not argue hard against swapping them.
- **Bush v. Gore** is Level 1 as the rule-of-law hinge: an election decided by a court on a
  5-to-4 vote. The live instrument does not have it. The alternative is Level 2.
- **The Iraq War span and the Afghanistan War.** Afghanistan (October 7, 2001 to August
  30, 2021) is a second cross-epoch span, twenty years long, across the End of History,
  the Interregnum, and the Reckoning. I have left it as a Level 2 point at its start for
  now, with the end event for the Reckoning corpus. The alternative is to make it a span
  alongside Iraq. It earns a lane by duration, and it would overlap Iraq from 2003 to 2011.
  This one is yours.
- **September 11 and the invasion of Iraq** are both Level 1. The first is the shock and the
  second is the choice, and the epoch's argument is in the distance between them. The
  AUMF (September 14) and the Patriot Act (October 26) are Level 2.
- **Katrina (2005)** is Level 1. The live instrument has it at Level 2. I promoted it because
  the rationale says "Katrina exposed state incapacity," and the epoch needs a Level 1
  event between the invasion and its end.
- **The impeachment of Clinton (December 1998)** is Level 2. It is the partisan war of the
  decade, and it is the least consequential of the epoch's constitutional events
  against *Bush v. Gore*.
- **The Republican revolution (November 1994)** is Level 2. The party takes Congress for
  the first time since 1954, and its significance is that Clinton triangulates in response.
  A case exists for the crown, which the spacing check might support.
- **The dot-com boom (1995 to 2000) and crash (2000 to 2002)** are carried as points, with
  Netscape's IPO (August 1995) at Level 3 and the peak (March 10, 2000) at Level 3.
  A span would duplicate the stock market's own story.
- **The surplus (1998 to 2001)** is Level 3. It is the order's proof of concept, and it
  vanished within three years.
- **The financial warnings:** LTCM's rescue (September 1998), Enron (December 2001), and
  Sarbanes–Oxley (July 2002) are Level 3. They are the order's earlier failures, handled
  as scandals and not as signs.
- **Executive power:** the Patriot Act, Guantánamo, the torture memos, and warrantless
  wiretapping are Level 2 and 3 points. This is a continuation of the thread Iran-Contra
  raised in the last epoch, and the corpus should note it without judgment.
- **Immigration:** IIRIRA (1996) is Level 3 and the failed 2007 reform bill is Level 4. The
  thread's next arc runs through the Interregnum.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens January 1, 1992 and follows Morning in America's close on
  December 31, 1991. The Los Angeles riots (April 1992) and Clinton's election
  (November 1992) are early beats, and Fukuyama's book (1992) names the epoch.
- **The end.** The corpus closes December 31, 2007. The Interregnum opens on January 1,
  2008. The first credit failures of 2007 (Bear Stearns's funds, New Century, BNP
  Paribas) are here, as the epoch's last beats, and the Bear Stearns rescue (March 2008)
  and Lehman (September 2008) belong to the next corpus. The recession's NBER start
  (December 2007) is carried by the overlay.
- **The Iraq War span** begins here (March 20, 2003) and ends in the Interregnum. The
  Interregnum corpus must carry its end events (the 2011 withdrawal).
- **The Afghanistan War** begins here (October 7, 2001) and ends in the Reckoning (August
  2021). The next two corpora must carry it, whether as a span or points.
- **The ratification signal.** Clinton's election opens the epoch, as the framework
  specifies: the opposition party accepts the ruling order's terms. The Iran-Contra
  pardons (December 24, 1992) close the previous epoch's thread and belong here by date.
- **The live placeholders** for NAFTA, welfare reform, 9/11, and Katrina are absorbed. The
  placeholders from 2008 onward are not touched.

## What the viewer should understand

- The End of History is the order at its apex, ratified by both parties and tested by four
  events in eight years.
- The boom was real and the gains were shared more widely for a few years than at any time
  since the 1960s.
- The financial deregulation and the trade opening of the late 1990s were Democratic as well
  as Republican, which is what ratification means.
- The state's competence and legitimacy failed in public, on a recount, a war, and a flood,
  before the economy failed.
- The belief in the end of history was sincere, and it lasted fifteen years.

## Handoff

*By the summer of 2007 the order's creed was already spent. The war in Iraq had gone
wrong, a drowned city had shown what the state could not do, and housing prices had begun
to fall. Two hedge funds had collapsed and a French bank had frozen withdrawals. The order's
own financial machinery was failing, and nothing stood ready to replace it. The next
question was what happens when an order's legitimacy ends and no successor exists.*
