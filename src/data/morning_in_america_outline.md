# Morning in America Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**Morning in America (1980–1991)** — Rise phase of the Neoliberal Order.

## Epoch identity

Morning in America is the epoch in which a new answer to the question of government
wins. The settlement that had governed since 1933 could no longer manage inflation,
stagnation, or its own coalition, and the country spent twelve years finding out whether
the alternative worked: lower taxes, tighter money, weaker unions, deregulated finance,
a larger military, and an open argument that government was the problem. By its own
measures it worked. Inflation broke, growth returned, and the Soviet Union ended. It
also left costs that fell in particular places, on steel towns, farms, unions, cities,
and the people who caught AIDS and crack, and a federal debt that more than tripled. The
epoch's name is a campaign slogan from a 1984 television ad. It is the name the
winners gave it, and it carries the irony that mornings end.

## Historical thesis

The Unravelling ended with the order's own central banker acting before the electorate.
Morning in America is the electorate catching up and a new order being built on the terms
of that decision. Reagan's coalition (Sun Belt suburbs, evangelicals, white working-class
defectors, business) won the argument that the New Deal's tools had failed and that
markets could do what government could not. **The policy is a package.** Volcker's tight
money breaks inflation at the price of the worst recession since the 1930s. The 1981
tax cut and the 1986 tax reform lower the top rate from 70 percent to 28. PATCO breaks
the unions' leverage. Deregulation and financial innovation open the savings and loan
industry, leveraged buyouts, and "shareholder value." A military buildup puts a pressure on
the Soviet economy that Gorbachev cannot meet. **The ratification is partial.** The
Tax Reform Act of 1986 was written by Democrats as well as Republicans, and Bush kept the
settlement while raising taxes against his own pledge. **The Cold War ends inside the
epoch.** The Wall falls in 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolves in December 1991, which
gives the order the proof it will lean on for the next fifteen years. The costs are real
and unevenly spread: AIDS, crack, farm foreclosures, and the S&L collapse run through the
same years.

## Foundational question

> Can markets and a smaller government do what the settlement could no longer do, and
> who will be left holding the cost?

The Unravelling's handoff names both clauses. The first is whether the new order delivers.
The second is the political question of who bears the transition, and whether those who
bear it can be outvoted, outspent, or persuaded.

Stress test: Volcker's recession, the tax cuts, and the deficit ask whether the economic
package works; PATCO, the farm crisis, steel, and the S&L collapse ask who pays; AIDS and
crack ask who the state protects; the buildup, SDI, Gorbachev, the INF Treaty, and the Wall
ask whether the Cold War strategy works; Iran-Contra and Black Monday ask whether the
order survives its own scandals and shocks. It fits.

## Historical answer

Largely yes on the first clause, and the answer to the second is that the bill went to
others. Inflation fell from about 13 percent in 1980 to about 3 percent by 1983. The economy
grew for seven years from late 1982, and the Cold War ended in what most Americans took to
be victory. The costs were concentrated: the unemployment of 1982, the farm and steel
foreclosures, the AIDS epidemic's first decade, a prison population that began its climb,
and a debt that the order did not address. Both parties came to govern inside its terms.

## Big-picture story

The decade began under Carter, and the order began before its president. In 1979 and
1980 Carter signed the airline, trucking, and rail deregulation laws, Volcker raised
interest rates toward 20 percent, and in April 1980 a rescue mission for the Tehran
hostages failed in the Iranian desert. In November Ronald Reagan, a former actor and
California governor, beat Carter in a landslide. On January 20, 1981, as Reagan took the
oath, Iran released the hostages. In his inaugural address he said that "government is
not the solution to our problem; government is the problem."

That summer he signed the largest tax cut in history, and when the air traffic
controllers struck illegally he fired more than eleven thousand of them. The recession
that followed drove unemployment to 10.8 percent by the end of 1982, and Volcker's
patience held. Inflation fell. The Cold War turned hot in rhetoric ("evil empire," Star
Wars) and in small wars (Grenada, Beirut, Nicaragua). By 1984 the economy was growing at
7 percent, and a television ad that began "It's morning again in America" helped carry
Reagan to forty-nine states.

The second term moved in two directions. At home, Congress and the White House rewrote
the tax code in 1986 with bipartisan support, a leveraged takeover boom reshaped corporate
America, and the stock market lost a fifth of its value in a day in October 1987 and
recovered. The Iran-Contra affair, secret arms sales to Iran that funded the Nicaraguan
Contras, nearly broke the presidency and did not. Abroad, Mikhail Gorbachev came to power
in 1985, and he and Reagan met four times. In December 1987 they signed the INF Treaty.

George Bush won in 1988 on "read my lips: no new taxes" and then raised them in 1990. His
presidency was the Cold War's end. The Wall fell on November 9, 1989, Germany reunified a
year later, an American-led coalition drove Iraq from Kuwait in a hundred hours in
February 1991, and Gorbachev resigned on December 25 and the Soviet Union dissolved the next day.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The turn, 1980–1983

Carter's last year and the first deregulation, the hostage rescue failure, Reagan's
election, the inauguration and the release of the hostages, the tax cut, PATCO, Volcker's
recession, the deficits, Social Security rescued, "evil empire," SDI, Beirut, and Grenada.

### Act II: The morning, 1984–1988

The landslide, Gorbachev, Geneva and Reykjavik, tax reform, Iran-Contra, *Challenger*,
Black Monday, the INF Treaty, the war on drugs, the AIDS epidemic, and Bush elected.

### Act III: The victory, 1989–1991

Tiananmen, the Wall, the S&L collapse, the broken tax pledge, the ADA, German
reunification, the Gulf War, the August coup, and the Soviet dissolution.

## Emotional arc

**Reversal → pain → recovery → confidence → scandal survived → victory**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Nine point events plus the end of the Cold War span. Nine is above the usual five-to-eight
target and is carried with a written justification, as in Reconstruction and the Ascendancy
(see below).

1. **Reagan elected** (November 4, 1980): regime change after forty-eight years of New
   Deal governance, the electorate catching up to the policy.
2. **Economic Recovery Tax Act** (August 13, 1981): the largest tax cut in history and the
   new order's signature act, which makes tax reduction the organizing principle of the
   party and the deficit the price.
3. **PATCO strikers fired** (August 5, 1981): Reagan dismisses more than eleven thousand
   air traffic controllers who had struck against federal law. The law is enforced
   against workers, and private employers learn that striking can be answered with
   permanent replacement.
4. **Reagan reelected** (November 6, 1984): forty-nine states ratify the new order's
   first term and the economy's recovery, and the "Morning in America" ad gives the epoch
   its name.
5. **Tax Reform Act of 1986** (October 22, 1986): the top rate falls to 28 percent with
   bipartisan sponsorship. It is the clearest case of the order's terms being written into
   the code, and of who got treatment under them.
6. **Iran-Contra revealed** (November 3, 1986): arms sold to Iran, in violation of an
   embargo, with proceeds diverted to the Nicaraguan Contras, in violation of the Boland
   Amendment. Executive power is exercised around Congress and the law, and the
   order survives it.
7. **INF Treaty** (December 8, 1987): the first treaty that eliminates a class of nuclear
   weapons, and the Cold War turning from confrontation to settlement.
8. **Berlin Wall falls** (November 9, 1989): the symbol of the Cold War ends, and the
   order's argument about itself changes from a defense to a victory.
9. **Soviet Union dissolves** (December 26, 1991): the rival is gone, and the Cold War span
   that began in 1947 ends.

Span: the **Cold War** (March 12, 1947 to December 26, 1991) ends here. It is owned by the
American Ascendancy corpus, and this corpus carries its end events.

**Why nine.** The editor's call, and it holds. PATCO and the tax cut together are the
era's story: who the order's rules were made for and who they were enforced against. Tax
Reform and Iran-Contra are the pair that shows the same thing from the other side, who
received a break and where the separation of powers gave way. A spine without any of the four
would be a narrative of the Cold War with a Reagan epigraph. The cost is two crowded pairs
(August 1981, and October to November 1986) and the Level 1 labels may thin out at the
widest zoom.

**Spacing check:** the gaps between Level 1 points are about 0.8, 0.0 (PATCO and the tax
cut, ten days apart), 3.2, 1.9, 0.0 (Tax Reform and Iran-Contra, twelve days apart), 1.1,
1.9, and 2.1 years. The longest is the three years from the 1981 pair to the landslide,
filled at Level 2 by the unemployment peak, "evil empire," SDI, and Beirut and Grenada. The
INF Treaty is the release valve: if the two pairs make the crown too dense on screen, it
is the first to drop to Level 2, since the Wall and the dissolution already carry the Cold War.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Reagan reelected (1984)** is Level 1 as the ratification of the new order, not just a
  second term. The alternative is the Level 2 the live instrument has, which would leave
  a five-year hole after the 1981 pair.
- **Iran-Contra invites the Watergate comparison.** Watergate ended a presidency and the
  order that followed survived Iran-Contra. The Level 1 note can say so without
  judging either. The pardons of December 1992 belong to the next corpus.
- **Berlin Wall and Soviet dissolution** are both Level 1, two years apart. The span
  already ends on the second, so the Wall could drop to Level 2. I kept both because the
  Wall is the moment the story changes and the dissolution is the moment it ends.
- **The AIDS epidemic (1981 onward)** is Level 2, as live. It is one of the epoch's costs,
  and its first reports (June 1981) and Reagan's silence until 1987 are the issue. It
  is cross-epoch and better suited to a thread (AE-16) than a span.
- **Volcker's recession and the unemployment peak (December 1982)** is Level 2. It is the
  price of the order, and the NBER recession overlay already carries it as a band.
- **The Gulf War (January to February 1991)** is Level 2. It is the order's display of
  military confidence, and the 100-hour ground war is the nearest thing to a victory
  parade the epoch has. It is seven weeks, so a point at its end date.
- **Iran hostage release (January 20, 1981)** is Level 3. The seizure was in the
  Unravelling and the release is the epoch's first day. The inauguration is Level 2.
- **Black Monday (October 1987)** is Level 2. The largest one-day fall in history did not
  become a depression, which the order took as proof of its resilience.
- **The Fukuyama essay (summer 1989)** is Level 4. "The End of History?" names the next
  epoch, and it belongs to this corpus by date.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **The Cold War (1947 to 1991):** owned by the Ascendancy corpus and ends here. The
  Berlin Wall and the Soviet dissolution are the end events.
- **The AIDS epidemic (June 1981 onward):** cross-epoch and runs through the next two
  epochs. Not a span, since its end is not in the corpus.
- **The war on drugs and the rise of mass incarceration (1971 onward, accelerating in
  1986):** a thread, not a span (AE-16). The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 is a point.
- **The recession of 1981 to 1982 (July 1981 to November 1982):** carried by the NBER
  overlay, so it is not repeated as a span.
- **The farm crisis (about 1980 to 1987)** and **the savings and loan crisis (1986 to
  1995):** real conditions, but the first has no clean edges and the second runs into the
  next epoch. Carried by points.
- **The Reagan presidency (January 1981 to January 1989):** already the president bars.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens January 1, 1980 and follows the Unravelling's close on
  December 31, 1979. The framework's rule is that the order begins with Reagan's
  election, but the year before it already carries the order's first acts under
  Carter (deregulation, Volcker, the draft registration, the Olympic boycott). The
  Unravelling's handoff and the live instrument's rationale both say the policy regime
  changed first.
- **The end.** The corpus closes December 31, 1991, and follows the Soviet dissolution
  on December 26. The End of History opens January 1, 1992.
- **The Cold War span** ends here, inside this epoch. My earlier note said The End of
  History must carry the Soviet dissolution, which was wrong: December 1991 falls inside
  this epoch's dates, so this corpus carries the end events, the Berlin Wall and the
  dissolution.
- **The Iran hostage release** (January 20, 1981) is here, as a point, and the seizure
  (November 4, 1979) was in the Unravelling.
- **The ratification signal.** The framework puts the opposition's acceptance at the
  start of the Apex (Clinton in 1992). The Tax Reform Act of 1986 and Bush's retention of
  the order's terms are the Rise's early signs, and the formal ratification belongs to the
  next corpus.
- **The Rodney King beating** (March 1991) is here, and the Los Angeles riots (April 1992)
  open the next epoch.

## A note on the rhyme with the Gilded Age

Kevin suggested early on that Morning in America might show a rubber-band effect against
the Gilded Age. The rhyme is real and worth saying carefully. Both are Rise phases that
follow a founding crisis and a rupture of the old settlement. Both run on low taxes,
weak unions, financial innovation, a legitimating story about wealth and merit
(Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth" and "greed is good"), and a deregulated rise in
inequality, and both end with a sense that the surface hides what is underneath. The
differences are as important: the Gilded Age had no federal bureaucracy to roll back, no
administrative state, and an open frontier. The new order is built against a state that
already exists and does not eliminate it (Social Security and Medicare were protected
in 1983 and never repealed). This is an argued claim and belongs in the epoch's rationale, not
in the Level 1.

## What the viewer should understand

- Morning in America is the epoch in which a new answer to the problem of government wins,
  and it won because the old answer had visibly failed.
- It is a package, and the pieces depend on each other: tight money, tax cuts, weak
  unions, deregulated finance, and a military buildup.
- The Cold War ending inside the epoch gave the order a proof that was partly
  coincidence and which it took as vindication.
- The costs were real and unevenly spread, and the order's winners and losers were
  different people.
- The name is the winners'. A reader who has lived through the epoch will remember
  it differently depending on where they stood.

## Handoff

*By the end of 1991 the order had won both its contests: inflation at home and the Soviet
Union abroad. The opposition was beginning to govern inside its terms, and the rival
that had justified a generation of American policy was gone. The costs of the win,
a tripled debt, an eroded labor movement, and the first decade of AIDS and crack, were
still unpaid. The next question was whether an order with no rival left could share its
gains, and which party would claim to be its steward.*
