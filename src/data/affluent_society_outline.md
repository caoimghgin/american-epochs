# The Affluent Society Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**The Affluent Society (1953–1965)** — Apex phase of the New Deal Order.

## Epoch identity

The Affluent Society was the moment the settlement worked. A country that had
spent a generation arguing about what government should do found the argument
over, because the opposition party accepted the New Deal's terms. Wages rose with
productivity, the middle class bought houses, cars, and televisions, and the Cold War
supplied a common purpose. It is the era later generations most often call
the golden age, and its own conscience was already asking who was standing outside
it. It ended with its greatest legislative triumph and, five days later, a riot.

## Historical thesis

The Ascendancy's victory is consolidated at home. Eisenhower, a Republican, leaves
Social Security and the regulatory state intact, expands both, and builds the Interstate
Highway System. Unions peak, productivity and wages rise together, and the GI Bill and
suburban housing build a mass middle class, with Keynesian management apparently
solving the business cycle. The Cold War turns inward: Sputnik drives a federal
investment in education and science, the missile gap and the Cuban Missile Crisis
bring the world to the edge of nuclear war, and the contest becomes a race to
the Moon. The settlement's second clause, who stands inside it, becomes the
decade's defining struggle. *Brown*, Montgomery, Little Rock, the sit-ins, Birmingham,
the March on Washington, and Selma force a Cold War-era government to extend its terms,
and the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act are their result. Kennedy's
assassination makes room for Johnson's Great Society, Medicare and Medicaid, and a
war in Vietnam that arrives as the consensus peaks.

## Foundational question

> Now that the settlement has won, can its plenty be shared with everyone, and will its
> terms be extended to the people standing outside it?

The Ascendancy handed forward a question about whether the order's plenty could be
shared and its terms extended. The Affluent Society puts it to the test at the moment of
greatest strength. The first clause is the economic question (distribution and the
quality of life, Galbraith's own concern). The second is the recurring American
question about who is a bearer of rights, now asked by the people who had been
excluded and answered, partly, by law.

Stress test: the Interstate Highway Act and the GI Bill's heirs ask how plenty is shared
and with whom; the suburbs and redlining ask who is inside; *Brown*, Montgomery,
Little Rock, the sit-ins, Birmingham, Selma, and the two civil rights acts ask whether the
terms will be extended; the War on Poverty and Medicare ask whether the plenty will be;
Sputnik, Cuba, and Vietnam ask what the Cold War demands in return; Hart–Celler asks
whether the 1924 logic of membership ends. It fits.

## Historical answer

Partly. The settlement extends its legal terms to Black Americans and to immigrants, and
begins to share its plenty through Medicare, Medicaid, and the War on Poverty. But it
extends them at the moment a war it cannot win is escalating, and the exclusions
it had built into housing, labor, and schooling prove harder to end than the laws
that barred them. The consensus is at its height and already fraying.

## Big-picture story

Eisenhower took office in January 1953, and in July he ended the Korean War. In
1954 his administration expanded Social Security to millions of new workers. The
Supreme Court's *Brown* decision that May ordered the end of school segregation,
and a year later a Montgomery seamstress named Rosa Parks refused to give up her
bus seat. In 1956 Congress passed the largest public works program in history,
the Interstate Highway System, and Eisenhower won reelection in a landslide.

Then the plenty turned out to have a cost. Sputnik in October 1957 broke the country's
complacency about science and schools, and Congress passed a federal education
act. Little Rock the same month showed that desegregation would require federal
troops. In 1960 four students sat down at a lunch counter in Greensboro, and a
movement began that put the South's exclusion on television. John Kennedy,
a young Catholic senator, narrowly won the presidency. His term brought the Bay of
Pigs, the Berlin Wall, and in October 1962 thirteen days in which the world came
close to nuclear war over Soviet missiles in Cuba. In 1963 Birmingham's police dogs
and fire hoses, the March on Washington, and Kennedy's civil rights speech built a
national moral consensus, and on November 22 Kennedy was shot in Dallas.

Lyndon Johnson used the shock to pass what Kennedy could not: the Civil Rights Act
in July 1964, a war on poverty, a landslide in November, and the next year Medicare,
Medicaid, federal aid to schools, and the Voting Rights Act. In August 1964
Congress had also given him a blank check in Vietnam, and in March 1965 the
Marines landed at Da Nang. In August 1965 Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act.
Five days later Watts burned.

## Three-act structure

### Act I: The settlement ratified, 1953–1956

Eisenhower's inauguration, the Korean armistice, the Social Security expansion, the
end of McCarthy, *Brown*, the Montgomery bus boycott, the AFL–CIO merger, and the
Interstate Highway Act.

### Act II: Plenty and anxiety, 1957–1962

Sputnik, Little Rock, NASA and the National Defense Education Act, Castro, the
sit-ins, the Pill, Kennedy's election, the Bay of Pigs, the Berlin Wall, the Freedom
Rides, *Silent Spring*, and the Cuban Missile Crisis.

### Act III: The consensus at its height, 1963–1965

Birmingham, the March on Washington, the *Feminine Mystique*, Kennedy's assassination,
the Civil Rights Act, the War on Poverty, Johnson's landslide, the Gulf of Tonkin
Resolution, Selma, Medicare, the Voting Rights Act, Hart–Celler, and Watts.

## Emotional arc

**Consolidation → plenty → anxiety → demand → triumph → crack**

## Level 1 narrative spine

Eight point events plus the Vietnam War span:

1. **Brown v. Board of Education** (May 1954): the Supreme Court rejects legal
   segregation, and the second clause of the foundational question becomes
   the epoch's defining struggle.
2. **Interstate Highway Act** (June 1956): the opposition builds on the New Deal
   state, and the physical form of the affluent society (cars, suburbs, trucking)
   gets a federal foundation.
3. **Sputnik** (October 1957): the Cold War turns inward. It breaks the era's
   complacency and brings federal money to science and schools.
4. **Kennedy elected** (November 1960): a generational turn that promises more of the
   settlement's terms, won on television by a narrow margin.
5. **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October 1962): the Cold War's closest approach to nuclear
   war, and the era's second great anxiety.
6. **Kennedy assassinated** (November 1963): the shock that makes room for the Great
   Society.
7. **Civil Rights Act of 1964** (July 1964): the terms are extended in law, the largest
   civil rights statute since Reconstruction.
8. **Voting Rights Act** (August 1965): the legislative apex of the consensus.

Span: **Vietnam War** (August 7, 1964 to April 30, 1975), from the Gulf of Tonkin
Resolution to the Fall of Saigon. It begins here and runs about nine years into the
next epoch. See the long-running conditions below.

**Spacing check:** the gaps between Level 1 points are about 2.1, 1.3, 3.1 (October 1957
to November 1960), 1.9, 1.1, 0.7, and 1.1 years. The longest gap is the three years before
Kennedy's election, which NASA, Castro, the sit-ins, and the Pill fill at Level 2 and 3.

**Contested calls to argue about:**

- **Watts (August 1965)** is Level 2, as in the live instrument. It is the crack at
  the moment of triumph and the epoch's closing image, and it falls five days after the
  Voting Rights Act, which would crowd the Level 1. A strong case exists for promoting
  it to mark the end of the epoch, as the Big Stick's closing event, the 1912 split, was
  promoted. I kept the crown on the apex and left the crack at Level 2.
- **Kennedy elected and Kennedy assassinated** are both Level 1. The election is the
  generational turn and the assassination is the pivot to the Great Society, and each is
  a distinct hinge. It is also a lot of Kennedy for one crown. The alternative is to drop
  the election to Level 2, which would leave a five-year gap between Sputnik and the
  Cuban Missile Crisis.
- **The March on Washington, Montgomery, Little Rock, and the Greensboro sit-ins** are
  Level 2. The movement's organizing events are the mechanism behind the 1964 and 1965
  laws, and I kept the Level 1 on the legal results. The thread is dense at Level 2.
- **Medicare (July 1965)** is Level 2. It is the Great Society's social-insurance peak,
  and the other candidate beside the Voting Rights Act for the apex. Medicare is the
  answer to the first clause of the question (sharing the plenty) and the Voting Rights
  Act to the second.
- **Social Security's 1954 expansion** is Level 2. It is the actual evidence of the
  ratification signal, the Republican administration accepting the New Deal, but it is
  less recognized than the Interstate Highway Act.
- **The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (August 1964) and Da Nang (March 1965)** are Level 2,
  with the Vietnam span behind them.
- **Hart–Celler (October 1965)** is Level 2 with extra weight. It ends the
  national-origins system the 1924 act built, and is the immigration thread's reversal.

## Long-running conditions (candidate spans)

- **Vietnam War (August 7, 1964 to April 30, 1975):** about ten and a half years. It is
  a cross-epoch span, owned by this corpus because it begins here, and it crosses the
  epoch end by nine years into the Unravelling, whose corpus will carry its end events
  (the Paris Peace Accords and the Fall of Saigon). The dates run from authorization to
  collapse, the American arc itself. This is a deliberate exception, as with the Cold War.
  The alternative conventions are the U.S. combat years (March 1965 to January 1973),
  which leave out the commitment and the end, and the Pentagon's own 1955 to 1975, which
  is nearly all of two epochs.
- **The civil rights movement (December 1955 to April 1968)** is a candidate cross-epoch
  span, about twelve years from Montgomery to the death of King. I have not included it,
  because it is better suited to a thread than a span (AE-16), and it overlaps with the
  Unravelling.
- **The Cold War (1947 to 1991)** is the live span owned by the Ascendancy corpus and
  continues here.
- **Not spans:** the postwar boom covers the epoch itself, and the space race (1957 to
  1969) crosses into the next epoch and is carried by point events.

## Boundary decisions

- **The start.** The corpus opens on January 1, 1953 and follows the Ascendancy's close at
  the end of 1952. Eisenhower's inauguration (January 20) and the Korean armistice (July
  27) are its first beats.
- **The end.** The corpus closes on December 31, 1965. The Voting Rights Act and Watts,
  five days apart, close the epoch, and the Time cover "We Are All Keynesians Now"
  (December 31, 1965) falls on its last day. Carmichael's "Black Power" speech (June
  1966) opens the Unravelling.
- **The Vietnam War span** begins here (August 7, 1964) and runs to April 30, 1975. The
  Unravelling corpus must carry its ending, the Fall of Saigon, as an end event.
- **Medicare and the Great Society** are here, even though their consequences run for
  decades.
- **The ratification signal.** The framework's rule that each cycle's Apex opens when the
  opposition accepts the order's terms is carried by Eisenhower's election (the
  Ascendancy's closing beat) and the Social Security expansion and Interstate Highway Act
  here.

## What the viewer should understand

- The Affluent Society was the settlement at its strongest, and its strength rested on
  a political consensus and a Cold War purpose.
- The plenty was real and was distributed through institutions (suburban housing, unions,
  the GI Bill) that also excluded.
- The civil rights movement was the struggle to extend the settlement's terms, and it
  succeeded in law and met limits in practice.
- The era ends at its height and the crack appears at once, in a riot, and in a war
  begun with a blank check.
- The golden age is a memory made afterward. The people who lived through it were
  already asking who was left out.

## Handoff

*By the end of 1965 the settlement had reached its peak: the opposition had accepted
its terms, Congress had extended them to Black Southerners and to immigrants, and the
plenty was being shared through Medicare and a war on poverty. But within days a
Los Angeles neighborhood was burning, and a war that had been authorized with a blank
check was growing. The next question was whether a coalition built on consensus could
hold the weight of the people it had left out and a war it could not win.*
