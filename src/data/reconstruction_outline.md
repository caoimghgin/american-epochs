# Reconstruction Editorial Outline

## Epoch

**Reconstruction (1865–1877)**

## Historical thesis

The Civil War destroyed slavery and preserved the Union, but Reconstruction
fundamentally remade the nation by establishing national citizenship and giving
the federal government new constitutional authority to protect individuals from
their own states. Congress briefly attempted to build and defend a biracial
republic in the former Confederacy. Political exhaustion, economic crisis,
organized white violence, judicial retreat, and electoral compromise ended
sustained federal enforcement, while the constitutional transformation survived.

## Foundational question

> Who is the bearer of rights, and who has the authority to recognize and protect those rights?

The original constitutional order largely assumed that states governed the civil
status of their inhabitants. Reconstruction confronted the fact that states could
deny membership—and therefore protection—to people living under their authority.
The Reconstruction Amendments nationalized citizenship and made individual rights
a federal constitutional concern.

## Big-picture story

The Civil War decided that slavery would end. Reconstruction attempted to decide
what freedom would mean.

First, slavery was abolished, but Southern governments attempted to restore much
of the old social order through Black Codes while President Andrew Johnson favored
rapid restoration. Congress responded by asserting national citizenship, placing
the former Confederacy under military Reconstruction, and constitutionalizing
birthright citizenship, equal protection, and Black male suffrage.

For a brief period, federal power defended new governments and Black political
participation against organized terror. That experiment then lost support. The
Panic of 1873 weakened the governing coalition, the Supreme Court narrowed federal
power, white paramilitary campaigns displaced Reconstruction governments, and the
disputed election of 1876 ended with the withdrawal of the remaining federal troops.

## Three-act structure

### Act I — Freedom without settlement, 1865–1866

Slavery ends, but the terms of freedom are unsettled. Johnson restores Southern
governments quickly; those governments enact Black Codes. Congress rejects the
idea that emancipation alone is sufficient and begins to define citizenship
nationally.

### Act II — The second founding, 1867–1872

Congress takes control of Reconstruction. Military districts, new state
constitutions, the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, Black officeholding, and
federal enforcement create the constitutional and political architecture of a
biracial republic.

### Act III — Retreat and abandonment, 1873–1877

Economic crisis, scandal, Northern fatigue, judicial restriction, and organized
white violence weaken federal commitment. Redeemer governments regain the South.
The election settlement of 1877 ends the sustained military defense of
Reconstruction.

## Emotional arc

**Emancipation → possibility → construction → resistance → retreat → abandonment**

## Level 1 narrative spine

This corpus intentionally uses nine Level 1 point events. The usual target is
5–8, but Reconstruction has two distinct late structural turns that should not
be collapsed:

1. Thirteenth Amendment ratified
2. Civil Rights Act of 1866
3. First Reconstruction Act
4. Fourteenth Amendment ratified
5. Fifteenth Amendment ratified
6. Ku Klux Klan Act
7. Panic of 1873
8. United States v. Cruikshank
9. Compromise of 1877 and troop withdrawal

The Panic represents the collapse of political capacity and attention.
*Cruikshank* represents the narrowing of constitutional enforcement. They explain
different mechanisms of retreat.

## Boundary decisions

- **Lincoln's assassination** remains the closing beat of *A House Divided* and is
  not duplicated as a Reconstruction point event.
- **The Thirteenth Amendment** is included because ratification occurs in December
  1865 and because abolition is the indispensable starting condition.
- **The end date** is April 24, 1877, when the last remaining federal troops left
  Louisiana's statehouse (South Carolina's left April 10); the political bargain was
  informal and unfolded through several decisions rather than one signed “Compromise”
  document. Corrected during reconciliation from an earlier "end of March" draft date,
  which undercounted by about three weeks against this same outline's own troop-withdrawal
  entries.
- **Long-running conditions** such as Presidential Reconstruction, organized white
  terror, and sharecropping are recorded as spans. Congressional Reconstruction
  was originally drafted as a fourth span but cut during reconciliation (2026-07-19):
  at ~10 of the epoch's ~12 years, it read as barely distinct from the epoch band's
  own boundaries rather than a bounded phase earning its own always-visible mark —
  unlike Presidential Reconstruction, which genuinely contrasts with what follows.
  The First Reconstruction Act point event already marks where it begins.

## What the viewer should understand

- Emancipation did not itself define citizenship or secure political equality.
- Reconstruction transformed the federal Constitution, especially the relationship
  between individuals, states, and the nation.
- Biracial democratic government was constructed and exercised, not merely imagined.
- Its destruction required organized violence, political retreat, and legal narrowing.
- Reconstruction failed as a sustained political settlement but endured as the
  constitutional basis of later civil-rights struggles.
